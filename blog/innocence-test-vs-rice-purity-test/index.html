Innocence Test vs Rice Purity Test: Are They the Same Thing?

If you searched for an innocence test and ended up reading about the Rice Purity Test, you are not lost. Most of the time, the two names point at the same list of 100 questions and the same score. But not always — and the differences matter when you compare your number with someone else's. Here is the honest breakdown.

Start with the simple version. The Rice Purity Test is a 100-item checklist of life experiences. You tick the ones you have had, and your score is 100 minus the number of ticks. A score of 100 means you ticked nothing. A score of 0 means you ticked everything. The list runs from mild stuff, like holding hands, to things most people never do. That is the whole machine, and it has worked the same way since the list settled into its 100-question form.

Now type "innocence test" into a search engine and you will mostly find the same machine wearing a different name. Same 100 questions, same scoring, same result. So why do two names exist at all?

The Short Answer: Usually, Yes — Same Test

In the common case, an innocence test is the Rice Purity Test. Sites use the softer name for a few plain reasons. "Purity" sounds moralistic to some people, and a site that wants a friendly front door would rather say "innocence". A generic name also avoids suggesting any link to a university. The test began at Rice University, as our history of the test explains, but the sites hosting it today have no connection to Rice. Calling it an innocence test sidesteps that confusion entirely.

So if a site calls its quiz an innocence test, gives you 100 questions, and scores you down from 100, you took the Rice Purity Test. Your innocence score and your rice purity score are the same number with a different label on top.

Where the Two Can Differ

Here is where it gets less tidy. Because nobody owns this test — there has never been a single official publisher — anyone can host a version of it, and versions drift. Three differences show up most often:

Question count. The classic list has 100 questions. Some innocence tests trim it to 50, or stretch it, or swap questions in and out. A score out of 50 is not comparable to a score out of 100, even though both call themselves a purity or innocence score.

Wording. The original list was written by students decades ago, and some of its wording shows its age. Modern hosts reword questions, soften some, and occasionally replace a few outright. Two people can therefore answer "the same" test and have faced slightly different questions.

Scoring direction. This is the sneaky one. Almost every version scores down from 100: more ticks, lower score. But a few themed variants score the other way, where a higher number means more experience. If you compare scores across sites without checking the direction, you can end up exactly backwards.

None of this makes one version "fake" and another "real". It just means the name on the door is not a guarantee. Before you compare your score with a friend's, check three things: how many questions, whether the scoring runs down from 100, and whether the questions look like the classic list. If all three match, the comparison is fair.

What Your Innocence Score Actually Means

Exactly what a rice purity score means — nothing more, nothing less. It counts how many items on one particular list apply to you. It does not measure character, kindness, maturity, or future choices. Our score meaning guide walks through every range, and the same ranges apply whatever the test is called: high 90s means very few items ticked, the middle of the scale is where most adults land, and the low end means most of the list applies to you.

There is no official scale, by the way. No university, publisher, or research body certifies what a 72 "means". Every scale you see online — including ours — is a reading of the list, built by looking at which items sit where. Treat any site that claims an official scale with suspicion. And if you want to know how your number compares with people your age, our average score by age page gives the usual self-reported ranges, with the honest caveat that no official dataset exists.

Is There an "Official" Innocence Test?

No. There is no official innocence test, just as there is no official rice purity test publisher. The list began as a student tradition, spread on paper, and then spread online, and every site hosting it today is an independent version. Some sites use the word "official" in their titles as marketing. It does not mean Rice University endorses them, and it does not mean their version is more accurate than anyone else's.

The practical upshot is simple: pick a version you trust, take it honestly, and compare scores only with people who took the same version. The name matters far less than the questions behind it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is the innocence test the same as the Rice Purity Test?

Usually, yes. Most sites use "innocence test" as a friendlier name for the same 100-question Rice Purity Test, scored down from 100. Check the question count and scoring direction before comparing scores across sites.

What is an innocence score?

An innocence score is the same number as a rice purity score: 100 minus the number of experiences you ticked on the list. A higher score means fewer listed experiences apply to you.

Is there an official innocence test?

No. There is no official publisher of either test. The list is a student tradition from Rice University, and every site hosting it today is an independent version.

Why do my friend and I have different scores on different sites?

Because versions differ. Different question counts, reworded questions, or reversed scoring all change the result. Compare scores only when both of you took the same version.

Which version should I trust?

The classic 100-question version scored down from 100 is the one most people compare. Take it honestly, and treat any "official" label in a site's title as marketing, not certification.
